Help/Deal tiers

What do Great, Good and Fair deal tiers mean?

A tier says how a listing’s asking price compares with what the land is worth. Where enough development sites nearby have sold, that is what they sold for. Elsewhere it is what a developer can pay at your costs and returns. Great is the lowest price relative to value. Every tier shows its reason.

The tiers

Where five or more development site sales recorded before today lie within a mile (or, where a mile holds fewer, within a mile and a half, then two miles), Arosa compares the asking price with what they sold for on this lot’s as-of-right floor area, the land value:

TierAsking price as a share of the land value
Great70% or less
Good85% or less
Fair100% or less

Above that is Full price. What a developer can pay sits beside the tier as a check. When the ask is more than it, the reason says the returns fall short; the tier stays where the nearby sales put it. When the building does not pay anything for the land even at Fair returns, the tier stops at Good and the reason reads “Doesn’t pencil at target returns.”

Where fewer development sites have sold nearby

The tier comes from what a developer can pay at three return targets, compared with the asking price per buildable square foot. A tier needs the ask to sit at or below what a developer can pay at that tier’s target:

TierRental yield on costCondo margin
Great8.5%28%
Good7.25%18%
Fair6.0%10%

Above Fair is Full price. These are Arosa’s starting targets; you can change them.

Either way, a listing with no asking price is Unpriced, and the building that earns Great or Good must also have unused buildable area of at least 1,000 square feet for Great and 500 for Good, unless the lot is vacant.

The reason shown

Each rating says what set it and names any condition that held it down. Where nearby sales set it, the site page reads, for example, “Deal tier: Good deal, the ask is 82% of what nearby development sites sold for.” Otherwise it is “set on what a developer can pay at your costs.” A condition holding the tier down follows, as in “Held at Good: flood zone.”

Conditions that cap a tier at Fair: a landmark or historic district, likely rent-regulated tenants, floor plates under 1,000 square feet, listing details that do not match city records, such as lot size, building size or an address range, and an asking price under a fifth of the land value, which may buy a unit or a share rather than the lot (“Asking price to confirm”). Conditions that cap it at Good: tenants or flood zone to check, city financing to confirm, the small-project mandatory inclusionary housing exemption, and a building that does not pencil at target returns where nearby sales set the tier. When Arosa cannot check tenants or the flood zone, that holds the tier at Good until it is verified. Unknown never counts as safe.

When there is no tier

  • The listing has no asking price (shown as Unpriced).
  • The listing does not match a tax lot.
  • The district allows no residential building as of right.
  • Fewer than five development sites have sold nearby and the building does not pay anything for the land even at Fair returns. The reason reads “Doesn’t pencil at target returns.”

Inputs and how fresh they are

Asking prices come from listings refreshed every six hours. Nearby development site sales are refreshed every night. Land value and what a developer can pay come from the method on its own page. Tenant and registration checks use HPD registrations and city records. All sources and refresh times.

Because tiers rest on land value, they move as nearby sales are recorded and whenever the land value method is refined.

How your own costs change it

Where fewer development sites have sold nearby, tiers are recalculated at your costs and your return targets, and the tier filter uses your numbers. Where nearby sales set the tier, your costs change what a developer can pay beside it, not the tier.

What it does not cover

A tier is a screen, not advice to buy. It does not weigh how negotiable a seller is, the state of title, or conditions you find on a visit.

Common questions

Why is a cheap listing only Fair?

A condition capped it. Open the reason: a landmark district, likely stabilized tenants or a data mismatch holds the tier at Fair whatever the price.
