Floor area matches city filings. Land value starts from what nearby sites sold for.

We compared Arosa's numbers with what developers actually filed with the city and what buyers actually paid. This page shows the results, the data behind them, and where they fall short.

Last run October 23, 2026

Holds up

Buildable floor area

For a typical new building in R6 to R10, the floor area filed with the city equals Arosa's figure at the median. Arosa lands within 10% of the filing 57% of the time, against 45% for lot area times FAR. Judged by the rules in force when each was filed, on filings that describe the lot as recorded, it is 63%.

Measured

Land value

Where five or more development sites within a mile have sold, Arosa's land value is what they paid per buildable square foot, adjusted to the lot. On sites set aside to test it, that figure was within half to double the price paid 89% of the time, and 78% of the time where those sales lay up to two miles away. What a developer can pay at a target return carries no accuracy claim.

Buildable floor area against filed new buildings

The test asks a simple question. If a developer had bought each lot as an empty site and asked Arosa how much floor area zoning allows, how close would the answer be to the building that was actually filed?

For each filing we divide the floor area on the filing by Arosa's as-of-right figure for the same lot. A ratio of 1.00 is a perfect match; 1.20 means the filing is 20% larger than Arosa's number. The comparison covers 2,647 new buildings in R6 to R10 districts, filed from 2014 to 2021.

The median ratio is 1.00: half the filings are larger than Arosa's figure and half are smaller. 57% are within 10% of it. The common shortcut, lot area times the residential FAR recorded for the lot, is within 10% for 45%.

Within 20% the two are close, 72% for Arosa and 68% for lot area times FAR. Arosa's advantage is in the tight band around the filing.

Where it misses, and why

Filing smaller than Arosa's figure

About 1 in 5 filings, more than 10% under

Developers often build less than zoning allows, because of budget, financing, the market, or a design that leaves floor area unused. Arosa reports what the lot allows, not what a given owner chose to build.

Filing larger than Arosa's figure

About 1 in 4 filings, more than 10% over

Community facility space can add floor area on top of housing in many districts. Projects built across merged zoning lots, or using transferred air rights, can also exceed what the single lot allows on its own.

These are the usual reasons for a gap. This check does not break each filing down by cause, so the shares above are totals, not attributions. For how Arosa derives floor area and which rules it applies, see how buildable area is calculated.

Judged by the rules when each building was filed

The comparison above holds filings from 2014 to 2021 against today's rules. A second check judges each filing by the rules in force on its filing date, before the December 2024 City of Yes changes, including Inclusionary Housing and Mandatory Inclusionary Housing where the lot was in a mapped area, measured on Arosa's own reading of the lot's geometry.

Of the 2,376 new buildings whose filing describes the lot as recorded, 1,497 (63%) were filed within 10% of the as-of-right floor area that zoning allowed on that lot when the building was filed.

Left out: 271 filings
166 state the floor area of a larger zoning lot than the one tax lot, with several buildings or tax lots filed together. 91 lots have a recorded zoning lot merger or a purchase of development rights. 7 have a City Planning Commission special permit and 7 a Board of Standards and Appeals variance or special permit. These do not describe the lot on record, so no lot-by-lot figure can be judged on them.
Why some filings can never match
Many owners build less than zoning allows: 419 of the 2,376, for example a three-storey building where five were allowed. Some buildings use floor area from merged lots or bought development rights that public records do not show, and variances and special permits change what a single lot may hold.
Sources
DOB new-building filings from 2014 to 2021; the Zoning Resolution as in force at each filing date (the Department of City Planning's archived text of October 2, 2024); City Planning's Inclusionary Housing and Mandatory Inclusionary Housing area maps with their adoption dates; ACRIS recorded documents; City Planning ZAP and BSA records.

Land value against what buyers paid

Arosa's land value leads with what development sites near the lot sold for: at least five such sales within a mile (a mile and a half where a mile holds fewer, then two miles), each priced per square foot of as-of-right buildable floor area and adjusted to the lot, then a distance-weighted median times the lot's own as-of-right buildable floor area. The test asks how close that figure comes to the price a buyer actually paid for the lot.

The test covers 430 development sites sold from January 2025, set aside and never used to shape the method. For each one, only sales recorded before its own sale count, so the figure is one a buyer could have seen on the day. 360 of the 430 had five or more such sales within a mile. For 89% of those 360, Arosa's figure was within half to double the price paid, and the median buyer paid 0.99 times it. Another 65 had five or more within two miles; for 78% of those, the figure was within half to double, and the median buyer paid 0.98 times it. The other 5 had too few earlier sales within two miles, and Arosa shows no nearby figure in that case. The test sites lie in Manhattan, the Bronx, Brooklyn and Queens; none is on Staten Island, where development site sales are fewest, so this test says nothing about figures there.

Closings used
Single-lot deeds of $250,000 or more, recorded from January 2025 to September 2026, where the buyer then filed for a new building or a full demolition on the lot.
Arosa's figure
The nearest earlier development site sales within a mile (a mile and a half where a mile holds fewer than five, then two miles), up to 15 and at least five, each priced per as-of-right buildable square foot and adjusted to the lot for its standing building, floor area ratio, existing apartments and size. Their median, weighted toward nearer sales, raised 19% on a corner lot and lowered 12.5% where the sales reach past a mile, times the lot's own as-of-right buildable floor area.
Within half to double
The price paid is between half and twice Arosa's figure. It is a wide band: a single site can still sit well above or below it.
A developer can pay
Beside the land value, each site shows what a developer earning the target return at your costs could pay, the residual. It is a check on the price, not a forecast of it, and no accuracy claim is made for it.

For how both figures are worked out and what feeds them, see how Arosa values the land.

How the floor area check was run

Every step uses public records, so the check can be repeated.

  1. Collect the filingsNew-building job filings from the Department of Buildings (NYC Open Data ic3t-wcy2), filed 2014 to 2021, with three or more proposed homes and a status of permit issued, plan exam approved or signed off. One filing per lot, the most recent. Filed floor area is the proposed zoning floor area on the filing.
  2. Match the lotEach filing is matched to its tax lot in PLUTO (64uk-42ks) for lot area and zoning district.
  3. Treat the lot as emptyArosa values the lot as the developer found it, with no building to keep, using the lot's mapped street widths from the Department of City Planning's Digital City Map where the district depends on them.
  4. Take the as-of-right figureArosa's standard as-of-right zoning floor area, before any optional bonus.
  5. CompareRatio is filed floor area divided by Arosa's figure. The shortcut is lot area times the residential FAR recorded for the lot in PLUTO, compared the same way.

How the land value check is run

The land value check follows the same pattern, using recorded sales in place of filings, and keeps half the sites apart from any work on the method.

  1. Find lots with a build intentLots with a new-building or full-demolition filing in DOB NOW since January 2025.
  2. Match the closingDeeds recorded in ACRIS that cover one lot only, convey the full interest, and show a price of $250,000 or more. The filing must come within 90 days before the deed was recorded or after it. One sale per lot, the latest before its filing.
  3. Set half asideSites are split in two by a fixed hash of the tax lot number. Only one half is used to work on the method; the results on this page are the other half.
  4. Price from earlier salesFor each site, the development site sales in Arosa's own records that were recorded before its sale, within a mile (or out to two miles where a mile holds fewer than five), are priced per square foot of as-of-right buildable floor area on their own lots and adjusted to the site. The weighted median of the nearest, at least five and up to 15, times the site's as-of-right buildable floor area is Arosa's figure.
  5. Fit on one half onlyThe four adjustments, the corner markup, the distance weighting and the step down for sales past a mile were chosen on the tuning half. The held-out half was scored once, at the end.
  6. CompareThe recorded price is divided by Arosa's figure. Within half to double means a ratio from 0.5 to 2.

What the figures do not mean

They are not an entitlement determination.
Arosa is a research aid. Verify any figure against current official text and a qualified professional before you rely on it.
A match across many lots is not a match for your lot.
The results describe how Arosa performs across thousands of buildings. Any single site can land well above or below the median.
Filings are not the legal maximum.
A filing records what the owner proposed, which can be less or more than what zoning allows for the lot alone.
The rules have moved.
Filings from 2014 to 2021 were made under the rules then in force. Arosa reads the rules in force today.

When this page changes

Both checks were last run on October 23, 2026. We rerun them whenever the floor area or land value calculations change, with the same method, and update the figures and the date on this page. Results from different runs are comparable because the method does not change between them.
